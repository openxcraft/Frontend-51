## Category overview

Three product classes surround this product, and none of them *is* it. **Patient-intake platforms** (Phreesia class) are the category's 20-year incumbents — "power everything around the visit" with intake as the wedge, sold to independent practices through health systems (Phreesia: 4,700+ organizations, "1 in 6 U.S. patient visits," vendor-claimed — q5/q28). **Generic form builders with a healthcare posture** (Jotform, Formstack, IntakeQ class) sell HIPAA-enabled intake/consent forms to anyone, priced per form volume or per practitioner add-on (q1/q2/q3/q6). **Practice-management suites** (Cliniko class) bundle intake forms inside scheduling + treatment records + billing (q21). patient-intake-forms is the clinic-owned bilingual intake instrument between them: builder + patient surface + review console, with the Arabic symptom vocabulary normalization no retrieved competitor ships, at clinic — not health-system — scale and price. The regional wedge is structural: MENA clinics run Arabic-first patient populations with English-charting staff (the Qatar mental-health study documents how "limited systematic language assistance" and "ad hoc interpreters" compromise "assessment accuracy, patient safety, and therapeutic relationship," with patients viewing "language-concordant care as central to protecting privacy" — q7); Arabic medical terminology variance is real enough that one disease family needed a multi-country consensus (q4); and Arabic questionnaire translation is a formal COSMIN-grade discipline (q30) — meaning bilingual medical forms are known-hard, and the category's answers (machine-translated templates, English-only forms with a bilingual staff member reading them aloud) are exactly the failure classes the region's clinics live with. No retrieved source quantifies a MENA intake-forms market size — none is claimed (honest gap).

## Indirect competitors (4)

- **The paper clipboard and the bilingual receptionist** — the true status quo at MENA clinics: English or bilingual paper forms, a staff member reading/translating for Arabic-first patients, manual re-keying into whatever system holds the chart. Free, universally understood, and structurally unable to capture colloquial symptom text as canonical vocabulary; the digitization pressure is structural (Formstack's paperless-onboarding marketing, q6; Phreesia's eForms acquisition, q28) even though no retrieved source quantifies clipboard transcription-error rates (research digest honest gap — we never cite one).
- **Machine translation pasted over English forms** — clinics running English forms and telling patients to translate, or staff translating ad hoc. The Qatar study's "ad hoc interpreters" finding is the human version of the same gap (q7); the Arabic-terminology consensus literature (q4, q12) and the COSMIN tradition (q30) both say medical instruments need deliberate bilingual authoring and validation — not auto-translation.
- **Practice-management suites generally** (Cliniko/Jane-class bundling) — the ceiling above: intake as one feature inside scheduling/notes/billing. We are the intake layer a clinic adopts without re-platforming; the suite's own pricing (per practitioner, q21) is the posture our pricing deliberately undercuts in scarcity terms (12 Q-3).
- **Symptom checkers / triage chatbots** — the counter-position, actively criticized in the literature (SCARF: a decade of under-controlled evaluations, q10/q17). We refuse the category's core move: our normalization maps vocabulary for a human review console; it never assesses, scores, or advises. The boundary is a marketing asset in a region wary of AI-as-doctor.

## Market and demand evidence (with sources)

1. **The category monetizes at clinic scale**: IntakeQ's intake-focused plans at $29.90–$84.90/mo with per-practitioner adds (https://www.intakeq.com/pricing, q1); Jotform's HIPAA-gated ladder to $99/mo (https://www.jotform.com/pricing/, q2); Cliniko's $45–$395 practitioner bands (https://www.cliniko.com/, q21). All vendor-listed, **unverified estimates** — anchors, not promises.
2. **Form craft is measured, not vibes**: guideline-compliant forms roughly double one-try error-free completion (78% vs 42%, Seckler et al. CHI as reported by NN/g — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-form-design/, q9); placeholder-as-label carries seven named harms (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/form-design-placeholders/, q8); error copy needs plain language, precise description, and a next action, and premature errors are "grading a test before the student has had a chance to answer" (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines/, q22). Our patient surface is built on these as law, not preference.
3. **Bilingual clinical instruments are a formal discipline**: COSMIN-based reviews and cross-cultural adaptation studies for Arabic questionnaires are an established literature (Europe PMC search, 2,786 hits — q30; e.g., Arabic Leicester Cough Questionnaire validation); Arabic medical terminology needed multi-country consensus for steatotic liver disease alone (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajg.2025.01.009, q4/q12). The gap between that discipline and a clinic's Tuesday intake form is the product's whitespace.
4. **Language-concordant care is a patient-safety concern in the region**: Qatar multilingual mental-health study — "inadequate interpretation compromised assessment accuracy, patient safety, and therapeutic relationship"; patients "viewed language-concordant care as central to protecting privacy and emotional safety" (Europe PMC, q7/q16). Saudi critical-care and linguistically-diverse-setting communication studies compound it (q16).
5. **Arabic symptom-text normalization specifically is thin literature** (229 and 5 hits on two searches — q14/q31): the deterministic-plus-assist posture is the honest engineering answer to a documented absence, not a marketing claim.
6. **Digitization context without a MENA TAM**: Saudi Vision 2030 digital-health narrative review — transformation central, evidence "dispersed" (q26); WHO — digital health application "remains uneven" (q24); DHA operates a live bilingual regulatory surface (q23). No source states a MENA intake market size — none claimed (honest gap).
7. **Terminology standards exist to stay lighter than**: SNOMED CT positions itself as "the global common language for clinical terms" (https://www.snomed.org/, q11) — our vocabulary is clinic-local and exportable, deliberately not a terminology-server binding (12 open question on a future standards bridge).
8. **PHI is a regulated class**: HIPAA Privacy/Security Rules structure the U.S. posture (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_Insurance_Portability_and_Accountability_Act, q13); Gulf frameworks exist (DHA, q23) but no specific e-form mandate was retrieved. Our spec carries jurisdiction-neutral PHI discipline (no PHI in titles/URLs, no answer-echo, retention as owner policy) without claiming any compliance status (honest, 12).

Read: the category already extracts $30–$100/mo from small clinics for English intake tooling. Our recommended posture (12 Q-3): per-staff-seat for builder/reviewer roles with unlimited patients, unlimited forms, unlimited submissions — patients are the point, metering them is the anti-pattern the category's form-volume quotas (Jotform's 100–10,000 monthly submissions, q2) would push us into.

## Positioning statement

For MENA clinics whose patients describe symptoms in Arabic and whose intake tooling was built for neither that language nor that work, patient-intake-forms is the bilingual intake instrument: a builder where every form publishes only when both locales are complete, a patient surface that reads Arabic-first RTL with one question per screen and resumable sessions, and a review console where free-text symptoms land on clinic-owned canonical vocabulary through an inspectable normalization — dictionary-deterministic at the core, assist-degraded-honest at the edge, and confirm-gated everywhere. Unlike intake platforms (Phreesia), it is clinic-scaled, money-free, and Arabic-native; unlike form builders (Jotform, Formstack, IntakeQ), the form is a clinical-linguistic instrument with a vocabulary, a review workflow, and an escalation flag — not a field palette; and unlike symptom checkers, it maps words for humans and refuses to judge. The clinic front-desk pair: clinic-appointment-desk owns the day's bookings; we own the intake artifact (LOOP-3, mirrored in 12).
