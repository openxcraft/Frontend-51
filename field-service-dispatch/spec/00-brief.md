# 00 — Brief: field-service-dispatch

Family: business_ops · Tier: 3 · Roster status: researched proposal, pending owner confirmation · Seed: none supplied.

## What it is

A bilingual (ar/en) dispatch board for service businesses: the owner or dispatcher runs the operating day as a board of **job cards** moving through an explicit, inspectable lifecycle — scheduled, customer-confirmed, en route, on site, in progress, work done, closed — with every transition recorded as an append-only event naming who, when, from where (board, technician phone, or customer touchpoint). The board is lanes by technician for the working day plus an unassigned lane for intake; a job card is created from a phone call, a website request, or a recurring visit; the dispatcher assigns a window and a technician by drag or by keyboard; the technician runs the day from a one-thumb mobile job-card view (check in, start, pause, complete) without ever needing the desktop. The product is a **declared non-AI product** (ai-native-patterns.md §1): dispatch is a deterministic state machine — no smart assignment, no demand forecasting, no "optimized" anything; the dispatcher decides, the machine guards the lifecycle's rules and records the truth. No AI affordance appears anywhere in the spec.

The unique feature is the **job-card lifecycle with customer confirmation touchpoints**. The customer-facing half of the lifecycle is modeled as first-class data: a booking confirmation request when the job is scheduled, a day-of reminder before the visit, an arrival notice when the technician is dispatched, and a completion sign-off when the work is done. Each touchpoint is a record with a state (pending, confirmed, declined, superseded, expired, unanswered), a channel (the abstract customer-messaging capability when bound, or a manual phone confirmation the dispatcher records), and an honest expiry. Two gates make the feature structural rather than decorative: a job cannot move to **en route** without either a confirmed touchpoint or an explicit, reason-recorded unconfirmed-dispatch decision, and it cannot move to **closed** without a completion sign-off (customer reply, or a staff-attested record when the customer is unreachable). A gate that can be waved through silently is a gate nobody trusts; here the bypass itself becomes data the job card carries forever. The evidence that this matters is the category's own literature: confirmation reminder practice is taught in FSM playbooks and template libraries (ServiceTitan's contractor playbook devotes a chapter to confirming appointments; "Reply YES to confirm" is the standard template idiom), and reminder mechanics are the best-evidenced no-show reducer in the research corpus (01).

The category gap is size-shaped (01 evidence). The Western FSM platforms are either small-business suites that bundle quoting, invoicing, and payments around scheduling (Jobber, Housecall Pro, Workiz class) or enterprise per-technician platforms priced and shaped for 50+ tech operations (ServiceTitan class — user sentiment: "too bloated for a 5-man shop," $245–$500 per tech per month, unverified estimates). The MENA service business between those stools — a 5–40 technician maintenance, AC, plumbing, electrical, or cleaning company whose daily reality is a WhatsApp group, paper slips, and a dispatcher's memory — is served by neither: the suites carry invoicing and payments scope it does not want (and LOOP-10 excludes here), the enterprise tier is priced for another world, and the regional on-demand platforms (Fanni-class) own the customer relationship and take the margin. This product is that middle company's dispatch desk: one board, one lifecycle, customer confirmations in the loop, bilingual end to end, priced for the owner who today prints nothing and remembers everything.

## Buyer

Owners, dispatchers, and operations managers of 5–40 technician service businesses in MENA: AC/refrigeration maintenance, plumbing and electrical contractors, appliance-repair services, facility-maintenance subcontractors, and cleaning/pest-control companies. The economic buyer and the daily board user are usually the same person at 5–15 technicians (the owner-dispatcher); at 15–40 a dedicated dispatcher or coordinator owns the board and the owner reads the day's outcomes. Secondary daily users: every technician (mobile job-card view — no desktop, no seat burden on their workflow), and the service supervisor or quality lead who needs a job's full timeline when a customer disputes what happened. Customers are actors, not users: they receive and answer confirmation touchpoints, and their replies land on the job card. Two to six dispatcher/owner seats; technicians participate without priced seats on the mobile view. Full personas in 02-user-research.md.

## Surface

Web application, desktop-first (≥1280px): the **dispatch board** (day lanes by technician + unassigned lane, drag-and-drop scheduling with keyboard equivalents), job-card detail (lifecycle timeline, touchpoints, notes), intake form, confirmation queue, customers, technicians, day sheet & jobs, settings. Responsive to 360px for read-and-act surfaces. Plus the **technician mobile job-card view** (≤767px, one-thumb, Arabic-first): my day as a stack of job cards with check-in / start / pause / complete actions and the next window always visible. Customer touchpoints are outbound artifacts (message via the abstract capability, or a copyable confirmation link the dispatcher can send through whatever channel the customer actually uses); no customer-facing app at v1. No native app; no public API at v1; no GPS tracking of technicians (LOOP-10).

## Unique feature

**Job-card lifecycle with customer confirmation touchpoints**, defensible on four grounds (evidence in 01): (1) the lifecycle is the product's law — a versioned, inspectable state machine whose every transition is validated and recorded, where competitors treat job status as a free-text/custom-workflow convenience (FieldPulse's custom status workflows are editable labels, not guarded transitions); (2) customer confirmation is modeled as data with states, expiry, and gates — the "Reply YES" convention the category teaches in playbooks becomes a structural gate on dispatch and closure, not a courtesy message; (3) the honest bypass — dispatching without confirmation is possible but must be recorded with a reason and remains visible on the job card forever, which is the difference between a guardrail and a cage for a real dispatch floor; (4) bilingual ar/en operation end to end — bilingual job cards, Arabic-first technician mobile, customer touchpoint copy in the customer's locale — served by no verified competitor in the category's comparison set.

## Exclusions (LOOP-10)

- No invoicing, quoting, estimates, payment collection, or pricing — the job card records work done and its timeline; money belongs to invoicing/accounting tools (the roster's invoice-generator-desk owns the artifact class).
- No route optimization, travel-time modeling, or GPS tracking — the board detects schedule overlaps for the same technician (simple interval arithmetic, honestly disclosed) and nothing more; navigation is the technician's own business; no location tracking of people.
- No parts inventory, stock counts, or warehouse management — parts needs are recorded as job notes and pause reasons (inventory-count-assistant owns counts; asset context is a plain-text reference, LOOP-3 with asset-register-tracker).
- No payroll, timesheets, or labor-rule compliance — attendance-shift-planner owns planned-time guardrails; this product's technician state is a manually-set duty flag at v1 (LOOP-3, mirrored in 12).
- No worker marketplace, no demand forecasting, no AI dispatch suggestions — declared non-AI; the dispatcher assigns, the machine validates and records.
- No team chat between technicians — communication is structured: job notes, state events, and the confirmation touchpoints. A chat surface would recreate the WhatsApp chaos this product replaces.
- No customer-facing web portal or booking page at v1 — intake is dispatcher-entered (phone/request channel → job card).

## Success signals (definition of done vs Mock Adapter)

An owner-dispatcher can: create a customer and a job card from a phone call; schedule it into a technician's lane with a window; watch the booking confirmation request appear as a pending touchpoint and confirm it (channel reply in the mock, or record a phone confirmation); hit the dispatch gate, be refused while unconfirmed, then clear it and dispatch the job to en route; walk the technician journey on the mobile view (check in, start, pause with reason, complete); record the completion sign-off and close the job; run a no-show job honestly to its terminal state; reschedule a job and watch the old confirmation supersede; read the full lifecycle timeline; export the day sheet and a job history; and see the confirmation queue's pending/expired items with phone-recording actions — all in the first session, on the Mock Adapter, with zero provider-specific knowledge anywhere in the UI and the full flow completing with every abstract capability unbound (phone-recording is the tested primary path).
