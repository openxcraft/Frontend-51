## Category overview

Three adjacent worlds collide in this product, and none of them occupies its slot. **Free government lookup portals** (the Saudi integrated-tariff inquiry service, the US HTSUS site, the UK trade-tariff service class) publish the authoritative schedules at zero price — single-jurisdiction, single-schedule, search-plus-browse, and nothing else: no saved work, no evidence layer, no cross-edition memory (q5, q12, q1). **AI classification platforms** (Zonos Classify, Avalara Tariff Code Classification, Gaia Dynamics, iCustoms-class) sell automated code assignment as an API or catalog batch — accuracy claims, per-order or enterprise pricing, aimed at e-commerce automation pipelines, not at a human desk defending a decision (q7, q8). **Enterprise trade-content databases** (Descartes CustomsInfo-class) aggregate HS codes, rulings, and regulations into paid global content — sales-led, priced for global supply-chain programs, far above a desk tool's budget shape (q4). And the **free consumer lookup engines** (findhs.codes, postal-operator tools, app-store HS apps) answer "what's my code" once, with no notion of a desk's persistent classification work (q1, q8, q39-digest).

The wedge this product occupies sits between all four: the desk officer's daily navigation surface — bilingual Arabic/English by construction (the official interpretive stack is English/French; Arabic exists only in national schedules — q13), attached to a curated ruling-notes library, with saved classification lists that survive national edition changes. The buyer's alternative today is a browser full of government tabs, a PDF of the schedule, and an email folder of rulings — which is exactly the spreadsheet-status-quo shape the portfolio has beaten in other families.

## Indirect competitors (4)

- **Government tariff portals** (the Saudi integrated-tariff inquiry e-service and its Arabic twin; the US HTSUS site; national portals generally): the free, authoritative incumbent every desk already has bookmarked (q5, q12, q14). Single schedule, browse-or-code-or-keyword search, no saved work, no cross-edition reconciliation, no ruling-notes attached to headings. This is the incumbent our deterministic spine must match honestly — search + browse — before adding anything.
- **WCO Trade Tools subscriptions**: the WCO's own online access to HS codes, Explanatory Notes, Classification Opinions, origin/valuation databases on subscription plans (q10). The authoritative interpretive layer — in English/French, at content-subscription pricing, and with zero desk workflow. It validates that the notes layer is paid content; it is not a desk surface.
- **The browser-tabs-and-folders status quo** (schedule PDF + portal tabs + saved ruling emails + a spreadsheet of "our codes"): free, flexible, and what every desk runs today. No reconciliation, no shared evidence, no Arabic/English parity — the manual alternative we formalize (and the thing national edition changes quietly break, q13/q16).
- **Freight-platform HS lookups** (Freightos-class tools bundling code lookup with shipping-cost estimation — q8): freight first, classification as a courtesy widget; the landed-cost kinship boundary with shipping-rate-comparator (12 Q-4) starts exactly here.

## Market & demand evidence (with sources)

- **The nomenclature is a big, structured object**: over 5,600 commodity groups at 6 digits (WCO FAQ [1]); national schedules extend to 8 digits (EU CN), 10 (US HTSUS), 12 (GCC integrated tariff — KPMG [2]); duty structure is ad valorem or specific per the GCC Common Customs Law [3]. A hierarchy this deep with national extension is a navigation problem before it is a search problem — the deterministic browse spine is a real product surface, not a fallback.
- **The official interpretive layer is EN/FR only**: Explanatory Notes in five volumes, English and French (WCO [4]); WCO official languages are English and French (HS Classification Handbook [5]); Arabic renderings exist only as national-schedule publications [2]. Bilingual AR/EN tariff navigation is a real gap no surveyed competitor fills — while the desk's document reality is bilingual by regulation: UAE import clearance requires commercial invoices in Arabic AND English [6], and certified translation demand for customs documents is its own services market [7].
- **National editions move on their own cadence**: the HS amends every five years (HS 2022 is the seventh edition [8]), but national schedules amend descriptions continuously — Saudi Arabia's integrated tariff schedule amendment of December 2025 introduced, deleted, and modified HS code descriptions and added code 98.04 [9], and the national conformity platform's tariff codes updated as of January 1, 2026 [10]. Desks that "know their codes" get silently stale — version reconciliation is unmet demand.
- **Rulings are the practitioner's defense layer**: the US publishes a free, official, Boolean-searchable rulings database (CROSS) and the ITA explicitly points difficult classifications to it [11][12]; Canada publishes advance rulings assigning 10-digit classifications [13]; Japan publishes searchable advance-ruling information [14]. The genre is mature, public, and — in every surveyed tool — disconnected from the code the officer is looking at.
- **The cost of a wrong code is real and practitioner-documented**: an incorrect commodity code found in audit produces a duty demand, interest, and potentially penalties (iCustoms [15]); US-regime practitioner guidance describes retroactive duty bills, penalties up to 4x the duty loss for gross negligence, and audits [16][17][18]; UK-regime guidance describes HMRC fines [19]. (Practitioner blogs, regime-specific framing — used as pain evidence, not as statistics we restate as law.)
- **Category spend exists at the automation tier**: duty/tax APIs priced per-order (one comparison blog cites a ~$2 + 10% of duties-and-taxes per guaranteed order model — unverified estimate [20]); AI classification sold on catalog scale [21]; the free tier is genuinely free everywhere [22][23] — so the desk-tool wedge must sell the workflow (bilingual navigation, evidence, edition memory), not the lookup itself.

## Positioning statement

For import/export desks clearing GCC-bound trade, customs-tariff-lookup is the HS-code navigator that reads the way the desk works: the nomenclature bilingually — Arabic national renderings beside the international English text, never auto-translated — the published duty reference with its structure and edition, and a ruling-notes library attached to the headings so every code choice carries its evidence. Unlike government portals it remembers: saved classification lists reconcile across edition changes instead of silently going stale. Unlike AI classification platforms it decides nothing: candidates come with provenance, the desk officer verifies, and the evidence pack speaks for the choice. And unlike every surveyed tool, it files nothing, brokers nothing, and guarantees nothing — it is the reference desk, not the risk.

## Complaints mined (paraphrased patterns)

- Desk-officer sentiment on free portals (q5/q12 practitioner guides, paraphrased): keyword search returns long undifferentiated candidate lists; the officer still walks the chapter tree and reads the notes to be sure — the portal is a data dump, not a reading surface.
- Arabic-side sentiment (q13 sources + bilingual-docs guides, paraphrased): the official interpretive texts are English/French; Arabic descriptions live in national schedules the officer opens in a second tab, and the two never render together.
- Compliance-lead sentiment (q6 misclassification guides, paraphrased): the defense against a classification challenge is the ruling trail — which today lives in email folders and printouts, not attached to the code.
- Procurement sentiment (q11 landed-cost guides, paraphrased): duty figures quoted around landed-cost conversations are schedule references repeated by word of mouth, with edition and caveat long lost — nobody can say which edition a number came from.
