## Category overview

Three categories collide at this product. **Certification-body platforms** (Certifable, Intact Systems, ARMATURE, ViaSyst, Azzule class) run the audit-and-certification cycle for conformity-assessment bodies of all kinds — applications, evidence, audits, decisions, certificates — and at least one (Certifable) markets a halal-specific configuration; these are the natural workflow homes a halal CB would buy today, and none of them, on any evidence found, joins an ingredient/formulation graph to the certificate register as queryable data (Q2, Q13). **Halal compliance software** (HIAS, Halal Practitioner's platform class) wraps halal-specific compliance tracking, deadlines, and dashboards — halal-domain tooling that targets the compliance workflow but, in the verified sources, centers document and deadline management rather than typed ingredient-to-certificate linkage (Q2). **The spreadsheet status quo** is the real incumbent: certification trackers-for-compliance-officers circulate as template downloads (Larksuite template, Q14), and practitioner sentiment describes manually tracking paperwork dates for every supplier certificate as the default condition of the trade (Q14, paraphrased). The wedge: a register whose renewal deadlines are computed ladders (per-scheme, staged, sourced conventions), whose formulations are typed graphs, and whose impact answers are set arithmetic — sold to the body that issues the certificates, not the brand that seeks them.

## Market & demand evidence (with sources)

- **The certification workflow is documented, staged, and audit-shaped**: application → document review → audit → decision → issuance, with a written audit report per audit delivered to the applicant [17][18]; the process includes "an opening meeting, document review, production plan assessment, traceability check, factory inspection" [19]; a 9-step procedure marketed as ISO 17065-aligned [20]; quotation-then-fee steps explicit in body workflows [21]. The product's typed stage ladder mirrors the trade's own documented stages.
- **Bodies are accredited, and the accreditation expects records**: CBs are accredited per ISO/IEC 17065 and accreditation bodies per ISO 17011 (draft export guidelines for halal meat certification, India DGFT) [22]; ANAB runs a halal accreditation scheme for CBs [23]; GCC accreditation documents require the halal CB to collect detailed applicant information [24]. The activity log and versioned policy data are not decoration — they are what an accreditation assessor asks to see.
- **Renewal is staged by scheme — the ladder is real**: general schemes renew with applications a minimum 45 days before expiry; Gulf-scheme certificates require renewal applications a minimum six months before expiry (practitioner comparison, LinkedIn) [25]; Sri Lanka's HAC publishes 45-day and six-month windows by scheme [26]; Indonesia's BPJPH grants four-year certificates with renewal applications due at least three months before expiry [27][28]; MUI certificates run two-year validity against HAS 23000 requirements [29]; typical validity reported at one-to-three years depending on body and product [30]; renewal end-to-end takes 4–8 weeks [31]. No verified source shows any software productizing these staged windows as per-scheme computed ladders.
- **Ingredient status is a live, contested data domain**: the halal/haram/mushbooh trichotomy governs industrial additives (E-numbered ingredients sit mostly in mushbooh, derivable from animal or plant sources) [32]; consumer tools ship E-number verdict databases [11]; academic work builds linked open data for halal food products and frameworks for tracing flavouring-ingredient halal status [33]. The CB's internal ingredient master data — with its own resolutions and supplier evidence — is the professional counterpart of this public activity.
- **Supplier-certificate expiry is the named operational pain**: expired vendor halal documents put the buying business's own compliance at risk; manual date-tracking across every supplier is the described condition [9]. Academic supply-chain integrity reviews repeat that traceability is essential and its absence the core failure mode [34][35].
- **The bilingual obligation is regulatory, not stylistic**: UAE food labels must be Arabic-only or Arabic/English, with Arabic mandatory, accurate, legible; production and expiry dates printed on the original label [36]; GCC labeling updates require bilingual labels with Arabic as one of the languages [37]; "English can be added but cannot replace Arabic" (practitioner summary) [38]. A Gulf-serving CB documents and certifies in both scripts.
- **Market size (honest, conflicting figures recorded)**: halal food market estimates diverge by definition — USD 1.09 trillion in 2025 at 4.7% CAGR to 2035 (GM Insights) [39]; USD 2.95 trillion in 2025 to USD 6.32 trillion by 2034 at 8.5% CAGR (ResearchAndMarkets) [40]; consumer spending on halal food near USD 1.3 trillion in 2021 (Halal Bureau citing state-of-the-industry data) [41]. The divergence itself is the finding: certification-driven supply chains are cited as a growth driver [42]. No auditable TAM exists for certification-body software seats; any number would be invented, so none is offered.

## Pricing landscape

Two different price universes must not be conflated. **The CB's own certification fees** (market context, not our pricing): average halal certification cost $200–$2,000 [43]; US application fee $250 non-refundable, per-location fees extra [44]; India fee structure around Rs 90,000 + Rs 2,000/product + Rs 20,000/year [45]; Australia $110 one-time application, $55 per additional certificate [46]; MUIS Singapore stages payments (new applications: 100% application fee + 40% certification fee at stage 1, 60% at stage 2) [47]; US annual renewal costs averaging $500–$3,000 [48]. These anchor what a certificate is worth to an applicant — the register's contents carry real money, which is the economic stakes for lapse-prevention. **Certification-body software pricing** is demo-gated across the verified set (Certifable, Intact, ARMATURE, ViaSyst — no public prices found) — any figure would be invented; adjacent per-seat SaaS bands (workflow/compliance tools in the $30–$100/seat/mo class) are the honest envelope (unverified estimate). Our posture (12 Q-3): per-seat for staff (owner/assessor/committee), free viewer seats, register-size tiers — priced against the value of a single prevented lapse, not against the CB's certification fees.

## Positioning statement

For halal certification bodies in and around the Gulf, halal-certification-tracker is the certificate-workflow desk that keeps the register honest from ingredient to expiry: every product's formulation is a typed graph walked on demand (a supplier's expiring certificate names the certificates it endangers), every renewal is a computed ladder of staged rungs per scheme (six-month, three-month, 45-day conventions as editable policy, not memories), and every decision, audit outcome, and edit lands in an append-only bilingual log the accreditation assessor can read. Unlike certification-body suites, it refuses audit scheduling and lab management to go deep on the register's own spine (LOOP-10 as positioning); unlike spreadsheet trackers, its deadlines compute; and unlike anything found, its traceability answers are arithmetic over data the body already owns.

## Complaints mined (paraphrased patterns)

- CB-operations sentiment (Q2/Q13 pages, paraphrased): workflow platforms bundle audit execution and scheduling the small halal body does not want to run — and still leave formulations as attachments nobody can query.
- Food-business sentiment (Q14, paraphrased): expired supplier certificates discovered late; manual date-tracking across every vendor document; the buyer's own compliance damaged by someone else's paperwork.
- Accreditation-facing sentiment (Q11/Q22 documents, paraphrased): the record-keeping expectations (application records, decision records, audit reports, traceability checks) are precisely what a shared drive cannot evidence cleanly.
